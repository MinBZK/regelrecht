## Context

The thing a jurist establishes and the engine executes had no name of its own.
The repository used at least seven words for it. In Dutch: *specificatie*,
*wetsbestand*, *YAML-bestand* and plain *wet*. In English: "law file", "law
YAML" and "specification".

Each of them fails somewhere.

*Specificatie* carries two meanings on the roadmap. One is the specification of
the language, the other is the machine-executable interpretation of one law. The
werkpakket `specificaties-i-documentatie-op-orde` recorded which word belongs
where as an open point.

*Wet* and "law" are wrong for part of the corpus. Of the 37 files under
`corpus/regulation`, five are a ministerial regulation or a municipal ordinance,
and so are six of the 81 in the demo corpus. The schema has thirteen regulatory
layers, and most of them are not a wet.

*Bestand*, "file" and "document" name how the thing is stored. What a jurist
signs off on is its content, and a regulation that changes gets a second file
while it stays the same regulation.

## Decision

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Dutch | het regelwerk | regelwerken |
| English | rulework | ruleworks |
| Frisian | regelwurk | regelwurken |

A **regelwerk** is one regulation as it is recorded in the law format: its legal
text, with the machine-executable interpretation beside it. It is identified by
its `$id`.

A regelwerk has **versions**. Each version is one YAML file, named after the date
its text took effect. A regulation that was amended once has one regelwerk with
two versions. Several regelwerken together are a *stelsel van regelwerken*.

A regelwerk is written in the law format as a whole: its metadata, its division
into articles, the legal text and the interpretation. The legal text is in it as
a verbatim quotation. The format carries that text and gives it no meaning; only
the machine-executable interpretation means something to an engine.

A regelwerk can be incomplete. The harvester writes one that holds only the
legal text, and enrichment adds the interpretation.

What a regelwerk is not:

- **The schema, the format or the language.** A regelwerk is written in the law
  format and conforms to the schema. The word never becomes a prefix for any of
  them.
- **The legal source.** *Wet*, *regeling* and *wettekst* keep naming what the
  legislature laid down: "this comes straight from the wet" stays as it is. The
  regelwerk is the project's record of that source.
- **The corpus.** That is the collection.

Code identifiers do not change. `law_id`, `ArticleBasedLaw`, the API routes, the
`law_entries` table, the WASM API and the canonical Gherkin steps keep `law`.
The word is vocabulary for prose and for user interfaces.

This RFC does not name the language.

## Why

### Benefits

- The double meaning of *specificatie* is gone. The word now means the
  specification of the language and nothing else.
- The name fits every regulatory layer, so an ordinance is no longer called a
  wet in the editor.
- It names the content. A regelwerk stays the same regelwerk when it gets a new
  version, or when it moves from files into a registry.
- It matches what the editor and the demo already list: one row per `$id`.
- *Regelwerk* is an existing Dutch word for a coherent body of rules, and read
  literally it says rules that work: they run, and what they compute takes effect
  in a decision. Whether the regelwerk itself has legal status is an open
  question; the werkpakket "Juridische status van een regelwerk" is about that.

### Tradeoffs

- "Rulework" is a coinage. English nouns ending in "-work" are often
  uncountable (paperwork, casework), so "a rulework" may make a native speaker
  pause, and the plural can be misread as a plant, like waterworks. A native
  speaker has not reviewed it yet.
- RuleWorks is also the name of an older production-rule language, a descendant
  of OPS5. The fields are close enough that a search will find both.
- *Regelwurk* is a calque that no Frisian translator has seen. It is on the list
  for the Frisian reviser.
- The demo's addresses now carry both coinages (`/regelwerken`, `/en/ruleworks`,
  `/fy/regelwurken`). An address is the hardest place to change a word later;
  the former paths redirect, and a later rename would add another set.
- Prose and code use different words for the same thing: "rulework" in a
  sentence, `law` in an identifier. Renaming the identifiers would touch the
  schema, more than 25 API routes, a database table and the WASM API, for no
  gain a user would notice.
- Accepted RFCs, RFCs by other authors and the published paper keep the older
  words. A reader who moves between those and the current docs meets both; the
  glossary row for rulework names the older ones.

### Alternatives Considered

**One file is one regelwerk**
- The first version of this RFC defined a regelwerk as one version: one YAML
  file.
- Rejected in review. The editor and the demo list, add, enrich and favorite by
  `$id`, so the word would have counted the wrong unit on nearly every screen,
  and a second word would have been needed for the regulation across its
  versions.

**Compounds on bestand or document**
- *Rechtsbestand*, *regelingbestand*, *regeldocument*, and the *wetsbestand* a
  few RFCs already used.
- Rejected: they name the storage, and the last one also says *wet*.

**Specificatie**
- The word the roadmap used.
- Rejected: it already means the specification of the language.

**Regelschrift, regeleditie**
- *Regelschrift* for the language with *regeleditie* for one instance.
- Rejected: *regelschrift* does not say whether it is the schema or an
  instance.

**Regelset**
- A set of rules.
- Rejected: a set is unordered, and it does not say what the set is of. The
  English "ruleset" falls with it.

**Regelalgoritme, rechtsalgoritme**
- Proposed because "rule-based algorithm" is a term in use for systems like
  this one.
- Rejected: "algorithm" frames a published interpretation of a law as a
  technical artifact, which is the framing the project argues against.

**Regelboekje, regelconfiguratie, regelnode**
- Rejected as too informal, too technical, or a metaphor in place of a
  description.

**Lezing**
- A reading of the law, which is accurate: a regelwerk is an interpretation.
- Rejected as the main term: on its own it is ambiguous, and it says nothing
  about being executable.

**English: the existing term unchanged**
- The docs said "law file".
- Rejected: it keeps both "law" and "file".

**English: regelwerk untranslated**
- Dutch domain terms usually stay Dutch in English prose.
- Rejected: this word is used in nearly every paragraph, and an English reader
  needs to be able to read it aloud.

**English: encoding, executable specification**
- The published paper uses both.
- Rejected: "encoding" names the act, and "specification" brings the double
  meaning back.

### Implementation Notes

- The glossary, the roadmap and the agent instructions use the word.
- The editor, the demo, the docs and the landing page use the word regelwerk.
  Screens a citizen reads in the demo (the portal, the loading text, the
  explanation of an outcome) keep *wet* and *regeling*: regelwerk is a word for
  the people who make and review them.
  The proofs of concept and their MCP tools still say *regelgevingsdocument*,
  the Dutch harvester screens still say *wet*, and the task skills under
  `.claude/skills/law-*` still say "law YAML".
- What a regelwerk for a bill is called, before there is a date of entry into
  force, is not settled here.
- The section on the top-level keys in `/reference/schema` is headed "The
  rulework". Its anchor did not change, so existing links hold.
- Werkpakket titles changed; their slugs did not. A slug is a reference that
  other pages and merged pull requests carry.
- The demo moved its tab from `/wetten` to `/regelwerken`, `/en/ruleworks` and
  `/fy/regelwurken`. The old paths redirect.
- The prose linter next to the `docs-writing` skill rejects the two older
  compounds on *bestand* and "file" in the docs pages, the roadmap and the
  landing copy, and rejects a compound of regelwerk with schema, format or
  language in everything it scans: the docs content, the RFCs, the roadmap and
  the landing copy. Components, the README and UI strings are outside its reach.
  It does not check RFCs for the older words, because an RFC is a dated
  document, and it cannot tell the two meanings of "specification" apart.
