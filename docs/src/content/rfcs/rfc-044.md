## Context

The thing a jurist establishes and the engine executes had no name of its own.
The repository used seven words for it. In Dutch: *specificatie*, *wetsbestand*,
*YAML-bestand* and plain *wet*. In English: "law file", "law YAML" and
"specification".

Each of them fails somewhere.

*Specificatie* carries two meanings on the roadmap. One is the specification of
the language, the other is the machine-executable interpretation of one law. The
werkpakket `specificaties-i-documentatie-op-orde` recorded which word belongs
where as an open point.

*Wet* and "law" are wrong for part of the corpus. Of the 37 files under
`corpus/regulation`, five are a ministerial regulation or a municipal ordinance,
and so are six of the 81 in the demo corpus. The schema has thirteen regulatory
layers, and only one of them is a wet.

*Bestand*, "file" and "document" name how the thing is stored. What a jurist
signs off on is its content, and that content stays the same thing when it
moves from a file into a database or a registry.

The language those files are written in had no name either. The docs call it
"the law format", the landing page *regelformaat*, the roadmap *de taal*, and
RFC-011 "RegelRecht YAML". A description is enough inside the project. It is
not enough for a second party that builds its own engine and has to say what
that engine implements.

## Decision

The language is called **Regelrechts**, the way the language of Nederland is
Nederlands. It is a proper name and stays the same in English and Frisian
prose: "written in Regelrechts". Regelrechts is the whole of the language: the
schema is its formal grammar, and the engine is one implementation of its
semantics. "The law format" and *wetformaat* remain as descriptions of it.

One regulation at one point in time, written in Regelrechts, is a
**regelwerk**.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| Dutch | het regelwerk | regelwerken |
| English | rulework | ruleworks |
| Frisian | regelwurk | regelwurken |

A regelwerk holds the legal text and its machine-executable interpretation side
by side. Today it is one YAML file. A regulation with two versions has two
regelwerken, one per date of entry into force. Several together are a *stelsel
van regelwerken*.

Three things are not a regelwerk.

**The schema, the format and the language.** A regelwerk is written in
Regelrechts and conforms to the schema. The word never becomes a prefix for
either of them. The schema is the schema of Regelrechts.

**The legal source.** *Wet*, *regeling* and *wettekst* keep naming what the
legislature laid down. "This comes straight from the wet" stays as it is. The
test for a sentence is whether "the YAML file of" fits in front of the word: if
it does, the sentence is about the regelwerk.

**The corpus.** That is the collection.

Code identifiers do not change. `law_id`, `ArticleBasedLaw`, the API routes, the
`law_entries` table, the WASM API and the canonical Gherkin steps keep `law`.
The word is vocabulary for prose and for user interfaces.

## Why

### Benefits

- The double meaning of *specificatie* is gone. The word now means the
  specification of Regelrechts and nothing else.
- A second implementer can say what it implements: Regelrechts, at a schema
  version.
- The two names form a pair a newcomer can hold: a regelwerk is written in
  Regelrechts.
- The name fits every regulatory layer, so an ordinance is no longer called a
  wet in the editor.
- It names the content and not the storage.
- *Regelwerk* is an existing Dutch word for a coherent body of rules, and read
  literally it says rules that work: they run, and they have legal effect. The
  project's own name plays on words the same way.

### Tradeoffs

- *Regelrechts* differs from the project name by one letter. Spoken aloud the
  two are hard to tell apart, and a typo or a search query merges them.
- The name ties the language to the project. An organization that adopts the
  language adopts a name that sounds like someone else's product, which may
  weigh against it as a neutral standard.
- *Rechts* also means right-wing in Dutch. The team judged that a pun someone
  makes once.
- "Rulework" is a coinage. English nouns ending in "-work" are often
  uncountable (paperwork, casework), so "a rulework" may make a native speaker
  pause. Artwork, framework and firework are countable, which is the pattern
  this follows. A native speaker has not reviewed it yet.
- *Regelwurk* is a calque that no Frisian translator has seen. It is on the list
  for the Frisian reviser.
- Prose and code now use different words for the same thing: "rulework" in a
  sentence, `law` in an identifier. Renaming the identifiers would touch the
  schema, about 25 API routes, a database table and the WASM API, for no gain a
  user would notice.
- Accepted RFCs and the published paper keep "law file" and "executable
  specification". A reader who moves between those and the current docs meets
  both words; the glossary maps one to the other.

### Alternatives Considered

**Regeltaal as the name of the language**
- The generic Dutch word for a rule language.
- Rejected as a name: it is the category. The roadmap already speaks of
  *regeltalen* in the plural, and RegelSpraak is one too. Regelrechts is a
  regeltaal.

**RegelRecht YAML**
- What RFC-011 called the language.
- Rejected: YAML is the notation, and the language is more than its notation.

**Wetsbestand, rechtsbestand, regelingbestand, regeldocument**
- Compounds on *bestand* or *document*.
- Rejected: they name the storage, and the first one also says *wet*.

**Specificatie**
- The word the roadmap used.
- Rejected: it already means the specification of the language.

**Regelschrift, regeleditie**
- *Regelschrift* for the language with *regeleditie* for one instance.
- Rejected: *regelschrift* does not say whether it is the schema or an
  instance.

**Regelset**
- A set of rules.
- Rejected: a set is unordered, and it does not say what the set is of. The
  English "ruleset" falls with it.

**Regelalgoritme, rechtsalgoritme**
- Administrative-law literature speaks of rule-based algorithms.
- Rejected: "algorithm" frames a published interpretation of a law as a
  technical artifact, which is the framing the project argues against.

**Regelboekje, regelconfiguratie, regelnode**
- Rejected as too informal, too technical, or a metaphor in place of a
  description.

**Lezing**
- A reading of the law, which is accurate: a regelwerk is an interpretation.
- Rejected as the main term: on its own it is ambiguous, and it says nothing
  about being executable.

**English: "law file" unchanged**
- Rejected: it keeps both "law" and "file".

**English: regelwerk untranslated**
- Dutch domain terms usually stay Dutch in English prose.
- Rejected: this word is used in nearly every paragraph, and an English reader
  needs to be able to read it aloud.

**English: encoding, executable specification**
- The published paper uses both.
- Rejected: "encoding" names the act, and "specification" brings the double
  meaning back.

### Implementation Notes

- The glossary, the roadmap and the agent instructions name the language
  Regelrechts. Existing prose that says "the law format" stays; the name is
  introduced where the language is defined, not substituted everywhere.
- The editor, the demo, the docs, the roadmap and the landing page use the word
  regelwerk.
  The proofs of concept and their MCP tools still say *regelgevingsdocument*,
  and the task skills under `.claude/skills/law-*` still say "law YAML".
- The heading "The law file" on `/reference/schema` stays, with its anchor. That
  page describes the schema, and a heading with the new word there is where it
  would slide onto the schema.
- Werkpakket titles changed; their slugs did not. A slug is a reference that
  other pages and merged pull requests carry.
- The demo moved its tab from `/wetten` to `/regelwerken`, `/en/ruleworks` and
  `/fy/regelwurken`. The old paths redirect.
- The prose linter next to the `docs-writing` skill rejects the older words
  outside frozen documents, and rejects the compounds everywhere.
