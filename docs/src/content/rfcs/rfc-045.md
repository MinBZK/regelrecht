## Context

The chronolex runtime of [RFC-044](/rfcs/rfc-044) speaks four rule languages. A stream definition says what a gram records. A lexostatus definition in `cel.yaml` reduces a chronicle with its own vocabulary (`filter`, `bestaat`, `som`, `kies: laatste`, `bevat`, `jaar_van`, `groepeer`). The `synthese` and `rijen` blocks of `proces.yaml` say which cell supplies which parameter, per article and per table row. The regulation itself is written in the law format and run by the engine. The start-up checks in `packages/cel/src/controle.rs` exist to hold the four together.

So one fact is written down three times. The date of a notice is declared in the stream, derived again in a lexostatus definition, and mapped once more in the synthesis before it reaches the article that reads it. Each copy can drift from the others, and only a start-up check notices.

The two middle languages also repeat what the law format can do. A lexostatus filters a list, tests whether something exists, adds up a field and picks the latest entry; [RFC-016](/rfcs/rfc-016) gave the law format `FOREACH` with `filter` and `combine` for those operations. A derivation in the reduction language has no type checker ([RFC-037](/rfcs/rfc-037)), no trace per element ([RFC-039](/rfcs/rfc-039)) and no conformance suite. It also lives in cell configuration, where a jurist does not look.

### Experiment A

Experiment A (25 September 2026, rebased 28 September) ran the reduction and the synthesis of the proof of concept as ordinary engine runs, on branch `exp/reductie-als-engine`. It is not for merging. Each lexostatus became a regulation whose parameter `grammen` is the chronicle, handed over by the cell in time order. `bestaat` became a `FOREACH` with `combine: OR`, `som` became `combine: ADD`, `jaar_van` became `DATE_PART`. Synthesis became `source` in the consumer's regulation, with the binding of regulation to cell as deployment configuration.

The outcomes matched. After the rebase, the eight register lexostatuses gave 180 cases and 386 compared values without a difference, on the starting state and on added grams that probe the edges (time zones, a removal, a correction, a merger of municipalities). The synthesis produced the same table in the same order and the same final amount. In the runtime, 12 of 13 lexostatuses then ran through the engine behind a switch, with a comparison mode that treats every difference as an error. The type checker found three nullability gaps the reduction language passes without a word.

It also showed what the engine lacks:

| Gap | Workaround in the experiment |
|---|---|
| An inner `FOREACH` cannot see the outer element | a child context inherits the parent's locals (one line in `context.rs`), against RFC-016 |
| No way to build a record | an object literal whose fields are evaluated |
| No "latest" | the cell adds a `volgorde`; "latest" is `MAX(volgorde)` in one output and a second `FOREACH` in the next |
| No local name inside a `FOREACH` body | the subexpression is written twice |
| "No gram" and "a gram without that field" are both `null`; a missing key is `VariableNotFound` | a helper output per derivation; the adapter writes every key, as `null` if needed |
| No `source` per element | each per-row lexostatus exists twice, once scalar and once vectorized |
| A `source` parameter cannot be `null` or an intermediate outcome | an extra regulation per computed input |
| At most 1000 elements in a collection | one chronicle per regulation, under the limit in the proof of concept |

The Rust that would go is about 2,300 lines of production code (`reductie`, `synthese.rs`, `rijen.rs`, most of `controle.rs`), 1,400 lines of tests and 600 lines of schema. The engine work to replace it is estimated at 400 to 700 lines. The YAML grows: the eight register lexostatuses went from 170 lines to 818. With the operations below the estimate is 1.5 to 2 times the reduction language.

It is slower (release build, 200 runs):

| Chronicle | Reduction language | Engine |
|---|---|---|
| 9 grams, 7 outcomes | 1.9 µs | 215 µs |
| 109 grams | 15 µs | 2.3 ms |
| 999 grams | 120 µs | 23 ms |
| 9 grams, 25 outcomes (a real lexostatus) | 4 µs | 466 µs |

In the runtime a reduction took 0.21 ms (median, release) against 2 µs, 50 to 100 times slower; a whole request was 2.5 to 3 times slower, because the engine runs of the acts themselves do not change. Every `FOREACH` walks and clones the whole chronicle, once per outcome.

### Where the reading lives

The experiment answered the question of language, not of place: its regulations carried the layer `UITVOERINGSBELEID` and a placeholder URL, with no actor behind them. Two rounds followed. On 28 September the form of a gram moved into the article that establishes the fact (`vestigt` under `produces.extensions.chronolex`, so Wpp 107 describes the decision gram and Awb 4:87 the payment), and the reading moved into the article that asks for it (`leest`). The creator of Chronolexografie agreed with the first. The second made a lexostatus of every reading article and put the query algorithm in the law, where no law puts it.

On 29 September the reading was placed with the keeper of the register, under the provisions that set up the register and allow it to be queried. That model is the decision below. It also drops the case: every gram gets an id and refers to the gram it belongs to.

## Decision

A fact from outside a regulation is a value from a source. The law says which register exists, who keeps it and who may ask; it never says how the register works. The query is an article in the implementing policy of the keeper, written in the law format and run by the engine. The consumer asks for it with an ordinary `source`. The deployment binds the register to a cell, or to an adapter in front of a legacy system or an API. The reduction language in `cel.yaml` and the `synthese` and `rijen` blocks of `proces.yaml` go once every lexostatus and every process has been migrated. §1 to §4 place the reading. §5 to §13 are the engine changes it takes, each argued on its own, since a law needs several of them outside the chronolex runtime. §14 says where a query runs.

### 1. Four layers for a source

Each part of a source sits where a text carries it. The register of party designations is the example.

1. **A law sets up the register and names its keeper.** Kieswet G 1 lid 1: an association may ask the central electoral committee for the Tweede Kamer to enter its designation "in een register dat door het centraal stembureau wordt bijgehouden". G 2, G 2a and G 3 give each central electoral committee a register of its own. The same article says what goes into it (the request in lid 3, the entry in lid 1 and 4, a change in lid 6, a removal in lid 7), which is `vestigt` on that article.
2. **A law gives the consumer the ground to ask.** Wpp 121a: "Op verzoek van de Autoriteit verschaft een op grond van de Kieswet ingesteld centraal stembureau haar informatie betreffende de aanduidingen die bij hem geregistreerd zijn …". The competent authority stays the Autoriteit; asking is a step outward.
3. **The keeper's implementing policy names the register and describes the query**: its parameters, its outputs and the search as engine actions.
4. **The consumer's policy fills in only what the law leaves open**, such as the key a register is queried on.

The keeper's policy, as it would look without a schema change:

```yaml
$id: beleid_centraal_stembureau_tweede_kamer
regulatory_layer: UITVOERINGSBELEID
competent_authority: {name: centraal stembureau voor de verkiezing van de leden van de Tweede Kamer}
legal_basis: [{law_id: kieswet, article: G 1}, {law_id: wet_op_de_politieke_partijen, article: 121a}]
articles:
  - number: '2'
    text: Het register van aanduidingen wordt bevraagd op de naam van de vereniging.
    machine_readable:
      execution:
        parameters: [{name: rechtspersoon, type: string}]
        input: [{name: inschrijvingen, type: array, source: {}}]
        output: [{name: aanduiding, type: string, legal_basis: {law: wet_op_de_politieke_partijen, article: 121a}}]
        actions: [...]   # the search: §5 to §9
```

The consumer asks with RFC-007 `source`, nothing new:

```yaml
input:
  - {name: aanduiding, source: {regulation: beleid_centraal_stembureau_tweede_kamer, output: aanduiding, parameters: {rechtspersoon: $statutaire_naam}}}
```

The deployment, and only the deployment, says what answers:

```yaml
registers:
  beleid_centraal_stembureau_tweede_kamer#register_van_aanduidingen: {cel: centraal_stembureau}
  beleid_kvk_handelsregister#handelsregister: {adapter: kvk_api}
```

The runtime registers a `DataSource` per entry with the keeper's policy as its `law_scope`, which the engine supports today. The engine passes the parameters of the query article to the data source as criteria, so an adapter receives `{rechtspersoon: …}` and returns the matching records. The search in the policy runs unchanged whether a chronicle or an API stands behind it. A test that puts a `DictDataSource` behind a fictional income register shows that neither law nor policy knows the difference.

The name of the register comes from the keeper, since the Kieswet says "een register". Until the schema has a place for it (Open Question 3), the law declares an open term and the policy implements it, the existing mechanism of [RFC-003](/rfcs/rfc-003).

This amends the provisional shape in RFC-022 §4.1, where a cell declares its lexostatuses in cell configuration, and RFC-044 §2, where the process configuration names cell, lexostatus and name mapping per source. It keeps what both decided: synthesis at the consumer, and no deployment name in a law.

### 2. Generic Awb duties: the authority's own article

Awb 4:52 lid 1 says "Het subsidiebedrag wordt overeenkomstig de subsidievaststelling betaald", and Awb 4:95 lid 4 that paid advances are set off against the sum still to pay. Neither says which administration holds the payments of one administrative body, and a generic law cannot. The entry point is an article in the body's own policy (or its own law) that names Awb 4:52 and 4:95 lid 4 as its `legal_basis` and carries them out through an ordinary `source` on its own payment administration. That administration rests on Archiefwet 1995 art. 3 and the payments Awb 4:87 establishes. The Awb gets no `open_terms` for it, and the engine does not choose an implementation by `competent_authority`.

With a fictional allowance: an advance decision of 500, paid, and a final determination of 300. "Paid under the determination" is 0; "paid on advances in the same application" is 500; still to pay is 0, and 200 was paid without being owed. Keyed on the determination alone, it would pay 300 on top of the 500 already paid.

### 3. Gram identity and references

There is no case. The cell gives each gram an id when it records it (a UUID v7, under the write lock, so ids rise in time and are unique across cells). A gram refers by id to the gram it belongs to, and the name of each reference comes from the text that establishes it:

| Reference | From → to | Text |
|---|---|---|
| `op_aanvraag` | decision → application | Wpp 107 lid 1 "besluit op de aanvraag"; Awb 4:19 |
| `aanvraag` | supplement → application | Awb 4:5 lid 1 |
| `besluit` | notification or payment → decision | Awb 3:41 lid 1; Awb 4:87 |
| `wijzigt` | decision → decision | Awb 4:48 to 4:50; RFC-022 §3.1 |
| `betreft` | decision taken ex officio → the decision it concerns | Awb 4:57 lid 1 (the name is our own) |

The establishing article declares the reference (`verwijst` next to `vestigt`), and the cell refuses a gram whose reference points at a gram that does not exist or was established by another article. A group follows from the references: the cell keeps an index from gram to root, and the root of a new gram is the root of the gram it refers to. The group has no name of its own and is called after its root, "application g-101". The creator of Chronolexografie agreed on 29 September 2026 that a gram refers to another gram. This removes `zaakkenmerk` and `besluitkenmerk` (RFC-044 §4), and RFC-008's rule that there is no case abstraction holds without the grouping RFC-044 still needed.

For the engine this means three things. The shape of a gram (§6, §12) declares `id` as its identity and each reference as a field. A query takes a gram id as a parameter, as the payment administration does with `besluit`. Following one reference is a filter; following two is a nested `FOREACH` that compares the inner element with the outer one (§5):

```yaml
# payments on advance decisions of application $aanvraag
operation: FOREACH
collection: $besluiten
as: b
filter: {operation: EQUALS, subject: $b.op_aanvraag, value: $aanvraag}
body: {operation: FOREACH, collection: $betalingen, as: p, body: $p.bedrag, combine: ADD, empty: 0,
       filter: {operation: EQUALS, subject: $p.besluit, value: $b.id}}
combine: ADD
empty: 0
```

The engine does not walk references recursively; the root is the cell's index and can be handed over as a field of each gram.

### 4. The text test

Every part of a binding (register, keeper, content, exit, key, name) gets one of three tags. **Text**: a law says it, with a quotation. **Policy**: a choice of the keeper or the consumer, written as machine-readable implementing policy with an owner. **Assumption**: nobody carries it. An assumption may stand in a proof of concept, visible and named. "The central electoral committee keeps a register" is text (Kieswet G 1 lid 1). "It is called the register of designations" is policy of that committee. "One cell keeps the registers of every central electoral committee" is an assumption of the deployment, since the law gives each committee a register of its own.

In a proof of concept we may write policy on behalf of an organization that has not adopted it. It is marked as such, with its own URL and the tag assumption, so it cannot pass for policy with an owner. When the real keeper later publishes its own interface, the binding may run partly outside regelrecht; the method allows that, because the law never named the implementation.

### 5. A nested FOREACH sees the outer binding

An inner `FOREACH` can read the `as` binding of every `FOREACH` it is nested in, in its `filter`, its `body` and its `let`. This replaces the scope rule of RFC-016 §Scope, which gave the child context an empty local scope and left `collection` as the only way across.

RFC-016 chose this on purpose. The rule was written for a law that sums over a household and then over its members, where routing the outer element through `collection` is enough. A query joins: for each row of an election result, the registration of the same list in the same area; for each advance decision, its payments (§3). The inner filter compares a field of the inner element with a field of the outer one. Through `collection` alone that means building the outer element's matches first, the same `FOREACH` in another place, and it fails when the join key is computed.

RFC-016 named the risk that one iteration's variable leaks into the next. Its text on flattened fields adds that a field name can shadow an outer name. Changing the rule adds a third: locals are resolved before outputs, definitions and parameters (`resolve_variable_internal`), so an existing law could change meaning without an error. They are covered this way:

- **Leaking between iterations.** Every iteration starts from a fresh child of the context the `FOREACH` is written in, and nothing is written back. Two conformance cases pin it: a variable bound in iteration *n* is not visible in iteration *n+1*, and sibling `FOREACH` operations do not see each other. They replace the two engine tests that pin the empty child scope, which the prototype set to `ignore`.
- **Flattened fields.** Only the `as` binding and `let` names are inherited. The fields RFC-016 injects as bare locals stay at their own level; an inner body reaches them only through `$outer.field`.
- **A silent change of meaning.** A binding may not shadow another visible name: an enclosing binding, a parameter, an input, an output or a definition. The type checker reports it and the law does not load. Before the change lands the checker runs over the corpus, and a law it rejects is renamed in the same change. The prototype, without this check, passed all 167 scenarios.

### 6. Records

```yaml
{operation: RECORD, fields: {gebied: $u.gebied, zetels: $u.zetels, inwoners: $inwoners}}
```

Each field value is a law-format value. A `FOREACH` without `combine` then returns a list of records, which is a table. The prototype evaluated every object literal instead; that would give a string such as `"$x"` inside an existing literal a new meaning. `RECORD` follows [RFC-004](/rfcs/rfc-004): one operation, a field name no other operation uses.

A parameter, input or output of `type: object` may declare its `fields`, and one of `type: array` its `items`. The type checker then knows the type of `$u.gebied`, which RFC-037 now leaves unknown, and checks a `RECORD` against the declared output.

### 7. The latest element

`combine` gains `FIRST` and `LAST`: the body of the first or last element that passes the filter, in the order of the collection. That replaces the two-step `MAX(volgorde)` construction with one operation.

The engine does not sort. An array input may declare the order its supplier guarantees (`ordered_by: [op_moment, vastgelegd_op]`). It is a statement about the input, not an instruction, and the engine does not check it. The runtime that binds the register does: a cell keeps its chronicles in that order (RFC-044 §5) and refuses to serve a query article that declares another, and an adapter states its order in the deployment. A `FIRST` or `LAST` over a collection without `ordered_by` is a load error, since "the last one" of a list whose order nobody states is not a legal fact.

An instant type with a `LATEST` that sorts in the engine is not chosen now. The order of a chronicle is already fixed under the cell's write lock, and an instant type touches every engine, the WASM build and the conformance suite (Open Question 1).

### 8. Local names in a FOREACH

`FOREACH` gains an optional `let`: names bound per element after `as`, in the order written, visible in `filter`, `body` and nested operations.

```yaml
operation: FOREACH
collection: $uitslagen
as: u
let:
  inwoners: {operation: FOREACH, collection: $inwonertallen, as: i, combine: ADD,
             filter: {operation: EQUALS, subject: $i.gebied, value: $u.gebied}, body: $i.aantal}
body:
  operation: RECORD
  fields: {gebied: $u.gebied, zetels_per_inwoner: {operation: DIVIDE, values: [$u.zetels, $inwoners]}}
```

A `let` name follows the shadowing rule of §5 and is a node in the trace. At the level of an article an intermediate value is an output, which is already named and traced, so `let` is only on `FOREACH`.

### 9. Absent, null and empty

A query must tell three answers apart: no gram at all, a gram whose field is empty, and a gram with a value. The experiment could not; both of the first two came out as `null`.

`FOREACH` gains an optional `empty`: its value when no element passes the filter, in place of the identity of `combine`. `empty` applies only then. A `LAST` that finds a gram whose field is `null` gives `null`; a `LAST` that finds no gram gives `empty`. With `empty` omitted on `FIRST` or `LAST`, the result is absent in the sense of [RFC-036](/rfcs/rfc-036).

```yaml
operation: FOREACH
collection: $inschrijvingen
as: g
filter: {operation: EQUALS, subject: $g.fields.aanduiding, value: $aanduiding}
body: true
combine: OR
empty: false        # not in the register: not entered
legal_basis: {law: kieswet, article: G 1, paragraph: '1'}
```

In the reduction language "no gram is no" was a sentence with a legal ground: the keeper keeps the register, so what is not in it did not happen. In the experiment it followed from `OR` over nothing being `false`, and for a text field `ADD` over nothing gave `0` where the law meant "none". `empty`, with the `legal_basis` a `FOREACH` already carries, puts the reason back where it can be read. It is required on a `FOREACH` over an input with `ordered_by`, which is how the loader recognizes a register; elsewhere the identities of RFC-016 apply.

A field that the `items` shape declares nullable and that an element lacks reads as `null`, not as `VariableNotFound`. An undeclared field stays an error.

### 10. Sources: the vector pattern, and null parameters

The engine does not get a `source` per element. A query that a consumer needs per row takes a list of keys and returns a list of rows, and the consumer joins them with §5 and §6. That keeps the number of calls to a register fixed per run, whatever the size of the table; a `source` inside a loop would fan one run out into a call per row, each with its own provenance and failure. The rebase measured the cost: each per-row query exists as a scalar and a vectorized article with the same filter. Whether that justifies a `source` per element is Open Question 2.

Two small changes to `source` are needed:

- A `source.parameters` value may evaluate to `null` when the target parameter is `nullable`. It is passed as the absence RFC-036 defines; a target that is not nullable makes it a load-time type error.
- A `source` without `regulation` (within the same law) may carry `parameters`, so an article can call another article of its law with a computed argument, such as a reference date.

### 11. The collection limit

`MAX_ARRAY_SIZE` (1000) becomes an engine setting with that default, which a runtime may raise; the runtime records the value in the receipt. The size of a register is a fact about a deployment, not a law. Raising the bound is not enough, because the cost grows with the register. The data source receives the parameters of the query article as criteria (§1) and returns only the matching records: a cell through an index on its chronicle, an adapter through the query of the system behind it.

### 12. Checks against the stream

The checks of `controle.rs` that tie a derivation to a stream become two checks in a row. In the engine, the register input declares the shape of a gram with `items` (§6), and the type checker checks every path a query uses (`$g.fields.orgaan`) at load time. In the cell, at start-up, the declared shape is compared with the stream definitions: every declared field exists on an event with the same type, every event named in a filter exists, and every field the stream records is read by a query or listed as not reduced, with a reason. For an adapter the second check falls to its own contract.

### 13. Trace and provenance

A query produces an engine trace like any other ([RFC-039](/rfcs/rfc-039)), so the path from gram to amount can be followed across register and consumer. An `items` shape names its identity field (`identity: id`); an iteration node carries that id next to the index, so the trace says which gram contributed. An element the filter skipped is recorded by index and id only. RFC-016 records the whole element, which for a register of a thousand grams and twenty outputs is twenty thousand copies in one trace.

The answer of a query names the keeper's policy and its hash, the register, and the number of grams at the time of reading, which in an append-only chronicle fixes the input. The legal ground of each derivation, now `grondslag` in `cel.yaml`, is `legal_basis` on the operation or output that computes it.

### 14. Where it runs

The query article belongs to the keeper. The runtime that serves the register runs it over that register and nothing else, and answers with its outputs; a query article has no `source` to another register, and the loader refuses one. For a register bound to a cell, the cell runs the engine on its keeper's query articles. That amends one sentence of RFC-044 §1, which says a cell never runs the engine on a regulation; it still never works across cells. For a register behind an adapter, the runtime that holds the adapter runs the query on the keeper's behalf.

Synthesis is the consumer's regulation with `source`. The process's runtime resolves each `source` to the runtime that serves the keeper's policy, keeps the provenance of each parameter, and reports a missing value per column as it does now.

## Schema and law format

| Change | Where | Schema |
|---|---|---|
| query input `source: {}` bound to a `DataSource` with `law_scope` | runtime | none |
| `legal_basis` on the keeper's regulation, on outputs and on operations | law | none (exists); not per article in `machine_readable`, which is closed |
| register name as `open_terms` in the law, `implements` in the policy | law | none (exists) |
| `vestigt` and `verwijst` under `produces.extensions.chronolex` | law | none (`produces` is open since v0.5.9); v0.8.0 should declare it |
| `let`, `empty` on `foreachOperation`; `FIRST`, `LAST` in `combine`; `recordOperation` | law | v0.8.0 |
| `fields`, `items`, `ordered_by`, `identity` on fields | law | v0.8.0 |
| `source.parameters` without `regulation`; `null` for a nullable target | law | v0.8.0 |
| `source: {register: <name>}`, or a `register` block in the keeper's policy | law | v0.8.0, if chosen (Open Question 3) |
| gram `id` and `verwijst`; no `zaak`, `besluit` or their identifiers | chronolex | v0.2.0 (v0.1.0 was never released) |
| no reduction in `cel.yaml`; no `synthese` or `rijen` in `proces.json`; most of `lexostatus.json` | chronolex | with the migration |

The scope rule of §5 changes the meaning of existing constructs, so v0.8.0 is a minor version. Everything else is additive.

## Performance

The engine is 50 to 250 times slower per reduction. For the proof of concept that does not matter; for a production register it does. Three steps, in this order, each measured on the benchmark above:

1. **No clone.** The engine iterates over a shared collection by reference.
2. **Criteria at the source.** The data source returns only records that match the parameters of the query (§11), from an index on (event, field, value). A comparison run with and without the criteria must give equal outputs.
3. **An index in the engine**, only if the first two are not enough: per run and per path, a map from value to element positions, built on first use by an equality filter.

The target is at most ten times the reduction language, and below 1 ms for one query. If step 2 does not reach that, this RFC is not accepted in this form.

## Why

### Benefits

- One rule language. A derivation is type-checked, traced per element and covered by conformance, and a jurist reads it in the same format as the article it serves.
- Every part of a source sits where a text or an owner carries it, and the text test makes an unowned part visible. Law and policy name no system, so a register can move from a cell to an API without a change to either.
- About 2,300 lines of Rust and 600 of schema go, against an estimated 400 to 700 lines of engine. A join, a table and a named intermediate also serve laws outside the runtime, such as AWIR article 7 lid 5.

### Tradeoffs

- The YAML is longer, 1.5 to 2 times the reduction language by estimate. The scope change reverses an accepted decision. The shadowing check and the corpus run must hold before it lands.
- The order of a register is a promise of its runtime that the engine takes on trust. `levert_aan` goes; a view from the register's side needs a reverse index over the consumers' `source`.
- A proof of concept holds policy nobody adopted. The marking of §4 is the only thing that tells it apart.

### Alternatives Considered

**Keep the reduction language.** Fast, short, and it names "no gram is no". Rejected as the end state: a second language for what `FOREACH` expresses, without type checking or trace, and synthesis in `proces.yaml` holds normative choices outside the law. What it does better is kept: `empty`, the start-up checks of §12, and its tests as the comparison set.

**The lexostatus as a regulation of the cell** (Experiment A). It works, but nobody owns it. The regulations carried the layer `UITVOERINGSBELEID` without an actor who adopted them, and the binding of name to cell lived in a mapping file.

**`leest` in the reading article** (28 September). It puts the query in the law of the consumer, which then prescribes how another body's register is searched, and it makes one lexostatus per reading article, or per paragraph. No law says how a register works; the keeper decides that.

**`source.rol` with a ground in `source`** (first note of 29 September). A role per input and a legal ground inside `source`, with a mapping of roles to cells in the deployment. It is a new source mechanism next to RFC-007 for something `source.regulation` to the keeper's policy already expresses, and it left the query with no owner.

**Inversion of control on the Awb.** Awb 4:52 would get `open_terms` for the amount paid and each body's policy would implement it. The engine selects an implementation only by municipality, province or water board code, so two national bodies would collide, and choosing by `competent_authority` is a new engine rule that touches every generic reading of the Awb. The body's own article (§2) needs none of it.

**Datalog or SQL over a bitemporal log.** Rejected in the review of 24 September: it replaces one second rule language with another. An instant type, object literals as records and a `source` per element: see §7, §6 and §10.

## Migration

The old and the new run side by side until the comparison set is green.

1. **Engine.** §5 to §11 land in law schema v0.8.0 with conformance cases, after the corpus is checked for shadowing.
2. **Comparison set.** The tests of the reduction language, the fictional cells in `packages/cel/tests/fixtures/cellen/` and the lexostatuses of every case corpus become a harness that runs each query both ways and compares every output. The switch of the rebased experiment (`dsl`, `engine`, compare) is the start.
3. **Keepers' policies.** The experiment's regulations become query articles in the policy of the keeper, on grounds from the text test, marked where we wrote them on the keeper's behalf. The deployment gains `registers`.
4. **Synthesis.** A consumer's regulation reads its facts with `source`. `synthese.rs` and `rijen.rs` go when every process is migrated.
5. **Gram ids.** Chronolex v0.2.0. An old application gram takes its `zaakkenmerk` as id; the runtime derives a fixed id from an old `besluitkenmerk` on load, so old chronicles stay readable. `zaakstand` becomes the state of a root.
6. **Removal.** When no reduction is left, `reductie`, the derivation checks of `controle.rs` and `lexostatus.json` go. The work queue comes last: it groups per root, which neither `FOREACH` nor this RFC offers (Open Question 1).

## Relation to the position paper

The paper puts reduction in the cell ("Reductie vindt altijd plaats ín de cel waar de betreffende chronolexogrammen zijn vastgelegd"), performed "op verzoek van een businessproces", and synthesis with the consumer ("Synthese gebeurt bij de afnemer, niet bij de bron"). Here the reduction is a query of the register, written by its keeper and run where the register is kept, at the request of a consumer's `source`. Synthesis is the consumer's regulation. The paper describes the derivation as "een afleidingsproces" and leaves its form open; the law format is our choice.

The paper says nothing about the identity of a gram or a reference between grams. The id and the references of §3 are our own; the creator of Chronolexografie agreed to them on 29 September 2026, and on 28 September to the form of a gram in the establishing article. The text test is part of our answer to the paper's question how actors can tell "wanneer wiskundige afleiding versus hermeneutische interpretatie van toepassing is": each part of a query is text, policy or assumption, and the tag says which.

## Open Questions

1. **Details of the engine language.** An instant type for a law that compares moments within a day (the fictional register had a notice at 08:30Z after one at 09:00+01:00). Grouping by distinct values, which the work queue needs. A test for "filled" (not null, not an empty text or list), now an `AND` of two `EQUALS` that counts whitespace as filled.
2. **A `source` per element.** The vector pattern covers the proof of concept at the cost of duplicate articles. A consumer that asks a different register per row would need a `source` inside a `FOREACH`.
3. **Is `register` a schema element?** Now the name comes through `open_terms` and `implements`, and the input is `source: {}`. A `register` block in the keeper's policy and `source: {register: …}` in the query would say it directly, at the price of a schema change.
4. **A second case.** Three problems from the 28 September round remain, to be tested on a second case: the order of the legal grounds in a gram still comes from the stream; the stage fields of a gram are ambiguous when one gram serves two procedures; and a work queue with filters of its own does not fit a query article yet.
