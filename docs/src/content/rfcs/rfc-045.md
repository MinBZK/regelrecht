## Context

The chronolex runtime of [RFC-044](/rfcs/rfc-044) speaks four rule languages. A stream definition says what a gram records. A lexostatus definition in `cel.yaml` reduces a chronicle with its own vocabulary (`filter`, `bestaat`, `som`, `kies: laatste`, `bevat`, `jaar_van`, `groepeer`). The `synthese` and `rijen` blocks of `proces.yaml` say which cell supplies which parameter, per article and per table row. And the regulation itself is written in the law format and run by the engine. The start-up checks in `packages/cel/src/controle.rs` exist to hold those four together.

The result is that one fact is written down three times. A field such as the date of a notice is declared in the stream, derived again in a lexostatus definition, and mapped once more in the synthesis of the process before it reaches the parameter of the article that reads it. Each of the three can drift from the other two, and only a start-up check notices.

The two middle languages also do what the law format already does. A lexostatus filters a list, tests whether something exists, adds up a field and picks the latest entry. [RFC-016](/rfcs/rfc-016) gave the law format `FOREACH` with `filter` and `combine` for exactly those operations. The difference is that a derivation in the reduction language has no type checker ([RFC-037](/rfcs/rfc-037)), no trace per element ([RFC-013](/rfcs/rfc-013), [RFC-039](/rfcs/rfc-039)), and no conformance suite, and that it lives in cell configuration where a jurist does not look.

### The experiment

Experiment A (25 September 2026) tried to run the reduction and the synthesis of the proof of concept as ordinary engine runs. The prototype is on branch `exp/reductie-als-engine` in this repository (commits `1fcf643a` for the engine and `83b3696d` for the cell), with a branch of the same name in the private case corpus the proof of concept runs (`e591f62`). It is not for merging.

In the prototype a lexostatus is a regulation whose parameter `grammen` is the chronicle, handed over by the cell as a list ordered on `op_moment`. `bestaat` becomes a `FOREACH` with a filter and `combine: OR`, `som` becomes `combine: ADD`, `bevat` becomes `IN` under `combine: OR`, and `jaar_van` becomes `DATE_PART`. Synthesis became plain `source` on the consumer's side, with the binding of regulation to cell as deployment configuration, the way RFC-022 §4.2 already describes it.

The outcomes matched. For all five example logins of the proof of concept, the engine produced the same 19 parameters as the reduction language, and the synthesis produced the same table and the same final amount. On the way the type checker found three nullability gaps (no designation, so no result and no reference date) that the reduction language lets pass without a word.

It also showed what the engine lacks. Three of the gaps needed engine changes that go against an accepted RFC or touch the law format:

| Gap | Workaround in the experiment |
|---|---|
| An inner `FOREACH` cannot see the outer element | a child context inherits the parent's locals (one line in `context.rs`), against RFC-016 |
| No way to build a record | an object literal whose fields are evaluated |
| No time ordering, no "latest" | the cell sorts and adds a `volgorde`; "latest" is `MAX(volgorde)` in one output and a second `FOREACH` on that number in the next |
| No local name inside a `FOREACH` body | the subexpression is written twice |
| No `source` per element | lexostatuses take a list of rows and return a list of rows |
| A `source` parameter cannot be `null`, an internal reference passes no parameters | an extra regulation computes the reference date |
| A collection may hold at most 1000 elements | one chronicle per regulation, still under the limit in the proof of concept |

### What it costs and saves

Measured at the base of the experiment, about 2,300 lines of Rust production code would go: `reductie.rs` (787), `synthese.rs` (613), `rijen.rs` (395) and some 500 of the 617 lines of `controle.rs`. With them go about 1,400 lines of tests and 600 lines of schema (`lexostatus.json`, 446 lines, and the synthesis part of `proces.json`). The engine work to replace them, with real operations, conformance cases and tests, is estimated at 400 to 700 lines. The prototype itself took 31 lines of engine and 130 lines of bridge in the cell.

The YAML grows. `registratiestatus` went from 76 lines of reduction language to 315 lines of law format, four times as long; a small fictional lexostatus went from 19 to 133. With a latest-element combine, records and local names, the estimate is 1.5 to 2 times the current length.

It is also slower. Release build, 200 runs:

| Chronicle | Reduction language | Engine |
|---|---|---|
| 9 grams, 7 outcomes | 1.9 µs | 215 µs |
| 109 grams | 15 µs | 2.3 ms |
| 999 grams | 120 µs | 23 ms |
| 9 grams, 25 outcomes (a real lexostatus of the proof of concept) | 4 µs | 466 µs |

Every `FOREACH` walks the whole chronicle and clones it, once per outcome, so the cost is outcomes times grams.

## Decision

Reduction and synthesis become engine runs over regulations in the law format. A lexostatus is a regulation the cell runs over its own chronicle; synthesis is `source` in the consumer's regulation, resolved by the runtime. The reduction language in `cel.yaml` and the `synthese` and `rijen` blocks of `proces.yaml` go away once every lexostatus has been migrated (see Migration).

That takes the engine changes below. Each is a proposal for the law format, and each is argued on its own, because a law needs several of them outside the chronolex runtime too.

### 1. A nested FOREACH sees the outer binding

An inner `FOREACH` can read the `as` binding of every `FOREACH` it is nested in, in its `filter`, its `body` and its `let`. This replaces the scope rule of RFC-016 §Scope, which gave the child context an empty local scope and left `collection` as the only way across the boundary.

RFC-016 rejected this on purpose, so the reason has to be stated. The rule was written for a law that sums over a household and then over its members, where routing the outer element through `collection` is enough. A reduction or a synthesis joins: for each row of an election result, find the registration of the same list in the same area, and the inhabitants of that area. The inner filter compares a field of the inner element with a field of the outer one. Through `collection` alone that means building a filtered list of the outer element's matches first, which is the same `FOREACH` written in a different place, and it does not work at all when the join key is computed. Legal text joins the same way when it takes, for each list that won seats in an area, the number of inhabitants of that area, and RFC-016's own argument that a reading belongs in the law applies to the join as much as to the filter.

RFC-016 named one risk: "a child context that inherited its parent's locals would let one iteration's variable leak into the next." Its text on flattened fields names a second, that a flattened field name can shadow something in an outer scope. Changing the rule adds a third, since locals are resolved before outputs, definitions and parameters (`resolve_variable_internal`), so an existing law whose inner body names a parameter that is also an outer local would change meaning without an error.

They are covered this way:

- **Leaking between iterations.** Every iteration starts from a fresh child of the context the `FOREACH` is written in, never from the previous iteration's child, and nothing is written back to the parent. What an iteration binds is gone when it ends, as now. Two conformance cases pin this: a variable bound in iteration *n* is not visible in iteration *n+1*, and two sibling `FOREACH` operations do not see each other's bindings. They replace the two engine tests that pin the empty child scope (`test_child_context_empty_local_scope`, `test_foreach_inner_scope_does_not_see_outer_binding`), which the prototype set to `ignore`.
- **Flattened fields.** Only the `as` binding (and `let` names, §4) is inherited. The fields of an object element that RFC-016 injects as bare locals stay at their own level. An inner body reaches the outer element's fields only through `$outer.field`.
- **A silent change of meaning.** A binding may not shadow another name that is visible at that point: an enclosing binding, a parameter, an input, an output or a definition of the article. The type checker of RFC-037 reports it, so the law cannot be loaded. Before the change lands, the checker runs over the corpus; a law it rejects is renamed in the same change, and the corpus scenarios have to stay green. The prototype, which inherited every local without this check, passed all 167 scenarios.

The bounds of RFC-016 stay as they are. A nested join multiplies, as a nested sum already did.

### 2. Records

A new operation builds a record:

```yaml
operation: RECORD
fields:
  gebied: $u.gebied
  zetels: $u.zetels
  inwoners: $inwoners
```

Each field value is a value in the law format: a literal, a variable reference or an operation. The result is an object. A `FOREACH` without `combine` then returns a list of records, which is a table.

The prototype evaluated every object literal as a record. This RFC chooses an explicit operation. An object literal is already a value in the law format, and the loader already has to tell an operation from an object that happens to have a key `operation`; evaluating the fields of every literal would give a string such as `"$x"` inside an existing literal a new meaning. `RECORD` follows [RFC-004](/rfcs/rfc-004): one operation, a field name (`fields`) no other operation uses, nothing evaluated that did not ask to be.

A record has a shape. A parameter, input or output of `type: object` may declare its `fields` (a list of fields in the usual form, each with name, type and nullability), and one of `type: array` its `items` (the type of an element, which may be an object with `fields`). The type checker then knows the type of `$u.gebied`, which RFC-037 now leaves unknown, and checks that a `RECORD` matches the declared output.

### 3. The latest element: ordering stays with the caller

`combine` gains `FIRST` and `LAST`: the body of the first or last element that passes the filter, in the order of the collection. Over an empty collection they give `null`, or the value of `empty` (§6). That replaces the two-step `MAX(volgorde)` construction of the experiment with one operation.

The engine does not sort. An array parameter may declare the order its caller guarantees:

```yaml
- name: grammen
  type: array
  ordered_by: [op_moment, vastgelegd_op]
```

`ordered_by` is a statement about the input, not an instruction. The engine does not check it. The cell does, at start-up: a lexostatus regulation must declare the order its chronicle is kept in, which RFC-044 §5 fixes as `op_moment` and then `vastgelegd_op`, and the cell refuses to start when a regulation declares something else. A `FIRST` or `LAST` whose collection is not a parameter with `ordered_by` is a load error, since "the last one" of a list whose order nobody states is not a legal fact.

The alternative was a timestamp type with a time zone (an instant) and a `LATEST` that orders on it inside the engine. It is not chosen now. The order of a chronicle is already a property of the cell, fixed under its write lock, and an engine that sorts again could only reach the same order or a different one. An instant type also touches every engine, the WASM build, the type checker and the conformance suite. A law that itself compares moments within a day would need it; none in the corpus does yet (Open Question 1).

### 4. Local names in a FOREACH

`FOREACH` gains an optional `let`: a map of names to values, evaluated per element after `as` is bound and in the order written, so a later name may use an earlier one. The names are visible in `filter` and `body`, and, by §1, in nested operations.

```yaml
operation: FOREACH
collection: $uitslagen
as: u
let:
  inwoners:
    operation: FOREACH
    collection: $inwonertallen
    as: i
    filter: {operation: EQUALS, subject: $i.gebied, value: $u.gebied}
    body: $i.aantal
    combine: LAST
body:
  operation: RECORD
  fields:
    gebied: $u.gebied
    inwoners: $inwoners
    zetels_per_inwoner:
      operation: DIVIDE
      values: [$u.zetels, $inwoners]
```

Without `let` the inner `FOREACH` is written twice, once for each field that needs it, and evaluated twice. A `let` name follows the shadowing rule of §1. Each `let` is a node in the trace, so the value a row was built from is visible.

`let` is only on `FOREACH`. At the level of an article an intermediate value is an output, which is already named and traced.

### 5. Source: the vector pattern, and null parameters

The engine does not get a `source` per element. A lexostatus that a consumer needs per row takes a list of keys and returns a list of rows, and the consumer joins them with §1 and §2. That is the pattern the experiment used, and it keeps the number of cross-cell calls per run fixed, whatever the size of the table. A `source` inside a loop would make one engine run fan out into as many calls to other cells as there are rows, each with its own provenance and failure. Whether a later need justifies it is Open Question 2.

Two small changes to `source` are needed for synthesis to live in the law:

- A `source.parameters` value may evaluate to `null` when the target parameter is declared `nullable`. It is passed as the absence RFC-036 defines. A target that is not nullable makes it a type error at load time, and an `unknown` still propagates as now.
- A `source` without `regulation` (a reference within the same law) may carry `parameters`. The experiment needed an extra regulation to compute a reference date because an article could not call another article of the same law with a different argument.

### 6. "No gram means no" is stated

`FOREACH` gains an optional `empty`: the value of the operation when no element passes the filter. It replaces the identity of `combine` in that case.

```yaml
operation: FOREACH
collection: $grammen
as: g
filter:
  operation: AND
  conditions:
    - {operation: EQUALS, subject: $g.name, value: aanduiding_ingeschreven}
    - {operation: EQUALS, subject: $g.fields.aanduiding, value: $aanduiding}
body: true
combine: OR
empty: false
legal_basis:
  law: testregeling_register
  article: '1'
  paragraph: '1'
```

In the reduction language "no gram is no" was a sentence in the lexostatus definition, next to a legal ground: the cell keeps the register, so what is not in it has not happened. In the prototype it followed from `OR` over nothing being `false` and `ADD` over nothing being `0`, which is the same outcome for a different reason, and for a text field it gave `0` where the law meant "none". Making it a field puts the reason back where it can be read, and `legal_basis`, which `FOREACH` already carries, states the ground.

`empty` is required on a `FOREACH` whose `collection` is a parameter with `ordered_by`. That is how the loader recognises a chronicle, and for a chronicle the meaning of an empty result is a legal statement a reader should see. Elsewhere it stays optional and the identities of RFC-016 apply.

### 7. The collection limit

`MAX_ARRAY_SIZE` (1000) becomes an engine setting with that default, and a runtime may set its own. The size of a chronicle is a fact about a deployment, not about a law, so the law format does not carry it. The runtime that raises it records the value in the receipt, so a result can be reproduced under the same bound.

Raising the bound alone is not enough, because the cost grows with the chronicle (see Performance). A cell hands a lexostatus regulation only the grams it can need: the grams of one case when the lexostatus takes a `zaakkenmerk`, and otherwise the grams that match the prefilter below.

### 8. Start-up checks against the stream

The checks of `controle.rs` that tie a derivation to a stream (that a filter names an existing event, that a field path exists on that event, that every recorded field is read or listed as not reduced) do not disappear. They become two checks in a row.

The first is in the engine. The chronicle parameter declares the shape of a gram with `items` (§2), and the type checker checks every path the regulation uses (`$g.fields.orgaan`) against that shape at load time. A typo is then a load error, where the prototype found it only at run time as `VariableNotFound`, or not at all when the field was absent on some events.

The second is in the cell. At start-up it compares the declared shape with its stream definitions: every declared field exists on an event of the chronicle with the same type, every event named in a filter exists, and every field the stream records is either declared and read or listed in `cel.yaml` as not reduced, with a reason. The checks on case identifiers and the reserved name `zaakstand` stay in the cell, since they concern the runtime and not a regulation.

### 9. Trace and provenance

A reduction run produces an engine trace like any other ([RFC-039](/rfcs/rfc-039)), and the path from gram to amount can be followed in one trace across cell and process. Two additions keep that trace useful for a chronicle:

- An `items` shape may name an identity field (`identity: vastgelegd_op`, or a gram id when the stream has one). An iteration node carries that value next to the index, so the trace says which gram contributed and the reference survives a later append.
- An element the filter skipped is recorded by index and identity only. RFC-016 records the whole element, which for a chronicle of a thousand grams and twenty outcomes is twenty thousand copies of the chronicle in one trace.

The receipt of a lexostatus answer names the regulation and its hash, the chronicle, and the number of grams in it at the time of reading, which with the append-only chronicle fixes the input. The legal ground of each derivation, now `grondslag` in `cel.yaml`, is `legal_basis` on the operation that computes it.

### 10. Where it runs

A lexostatus regulation belongs to the cell whose chronicle it reduces. The cell runs the engine on it, over its own chronicle and nothing else, and answers with its outputs. This amends one sentence of RFC-044 §1, which says a cell never runs the engine on a regulation: it now does for its own lexostatuses, and still never across cells. A lexostatus regulation has no `source` to another cell; the loader in the cell refuses one.

Synthesis is the consumer's regulation with `source`, resolved by the process's runtime to the cell that holds the lexostatus. Which cell that is stays deployment configuration of the process, as RFC-044 §2 has it. The mapping of names between source and consumer, now a block in `proces.yaml`, becomes the `output` and `parameters` of that `source`.

## Schema and law format

The additions go into law schema **v0.8.0**. The scope rule of §1 changes the meaning of existing constructs, so this is a minor version and not a patch. Everything else is additive.

| Where | Change |
|---|---|
| `foreachOperation` | `let` (map of name to value), `empty` (value) |
| `combine` enum | `FIRST`, `LAST` |
| `operation` union | `recordOperation`: `operation: RECORD`, `fields` (map of name to value) |
| `baseField` | `fields` for `type: object`, `items` for `type: array`, `ordered_by` for an array parameter, `identity` inside an `items` shape |
| `source` | `parameters` allowed without `regulation`; a nullable target accepts `null` |

The chronolex schemas (`schema/chronolex/v0.1.0/`) change with the migration: a lexostatus entry in `cel.yaml` names a regulation instead of a `reduction`, keeps `niet_gereduceerd`, and loses `afleidingen`; `proces.json` loses `synthese` and `rijen`. That version is unreleased and changes in place (RFC-044 Open Question 6). Once the reduction language is gone, most of `lexostatus.json` goes with it.

## Performance

The experiment is 100 to 200 times slower than the reduction language. For the proof of concept that does not matter. For a production chronicle it does. The plan has three steps, in this order, each measured against the same benchmark (the four chronicles above, release build, 200 runs):

1. **No clone.** The engine iterates over a shared collection by reference. The prototype clones the chronicle into every child context.
2. **Prefilter in the cell.** The cell derives from a lexostatus regulation which events it reads and which equality conditions every `FOREACH` over the chronicle shares, such as `$g.name` against a literal, or a field against an input parameter, and hands over only the grams that pass. It uses an index on (event, field, value) in the chronicle store; the review of 24 September already asked for an in-memory index per case. The derivation is conservative: a condition that is not in every `FOREACH` over the chronicle is not used. The comparison set (see Migration) runs each lexostatus with and without the prefilter and requires equal outcomes.
3. **An index in the engine**, only if the first two are not enough: per run, per collection and per path, a map from value to element positions, built on first use by an equality filter.

The target is at most ten times the reduction language on each benchmark, and below 1 ms for the lexostatus of one case. If step 2 does not reach that, this RFC is not accepted in this form.

## Why

### Benefits

- One rule language. A derivation is checked by the type checker, traced per element and covered by the conformance suite, and a jurist reads it in the same format as the article it serves.
- One place per fact. The stream records a field, the lexostatus regulation declares and reads it, and the consumer asks for it with `source`. The cell checks the first two against each other; the engine checks the last two.
- About 2,300 lines of Rust and 600 lines of schema go, against an estimated 400 to 700 lines of engine.
- The additions are useful outside the runtime. A join, a table and a named intermediate appear in laws that have nothing to do with chronicles, such as AWIR article 7 lid 5, which RFC-016 names as the next candidate for `filter`.

### Tradeoffs

- The YAML is longer, 1.5 to 2 times the reduction language by estimate.
- The scope change reverses an accepted decision and can change the meaning of an existing law. The shadowing check and the corpus run must hold before it lands.
- The order of a chronicle is a promise of the cell that the engine takes on trust.
- `levert_aan` (which lexostatus feeds which article) disappears; the consumer's `source` says the same thing from the other side, and a view from the cell's side needs a reverse index over the loaded regulations.
- A regulation per lexostatus is a law-format document for something the reduction language said in a few lines of configuration. Where it lives and who publishes it is Open Question 5.

### Alternatives Considered

**Keep the reduction language.** It is fast, short and it states "no gram is no" by name. Its start-up checks are strong. Rejected as the end state, because it is a second language for what `FOREACH` already expresses, without type checking, trace or conformance, and because synthesis in `proces.yaml` holds normative choices (which parameter comes from which fact) outside the law. What it does better is taken over: `empty` makes the "no" explicit, `items` and the cell's check keep the start-up checks, and the tests become the comparison set.

**Datalog or SQL over a bitemporal log.** The alternatives study in the review of 24 September considered a bitemporal log queried in Datalog or SQL, with PROV-O for provenance. Rejected as a whole there, since it replaces a second rule language with a different second rule language. Two parts were taken over: `vastgelegd_op` next to `op_moment` (RFC-044 §3) and PROV-O as an export format.

**Ordering in the engine with an instant type.** A `datetime` type with a time zone and `LATEST` ordering on it. Rejected for now, see §3; it stays open for a law that compares moments within a day.

**Object literals as records.** What the prototype did. Rejected for `RECORD`, see §2.

**A `source` per element.** Rejected for now, see §5.

## Migration

The reduction language goes step by step, with the old and the new side by side until the comparison set is green.

1. **Engine.** The changes of §1 to §7 land in law schema v0.8.0 with conformance cases, including the two scope cases of §1. The corpus is checked for shadowing first.
2. **Comparison set.** The existing tests of the reduction language (`packages/cel/src/reductie` and `packages/cel/tests`), the lexostatuses of the fictional test cells in `packages/cel/tests/fixtures/cellen/`, and the lexostatuses of every case corpus become a harness that runs each lexostatus both ways over the same chronicle and the same inputs and compares every output. `packages/cel/tests/exp_reductie_als_engine.rs` on the experiment branch is the start of it.
3. **Synthesis first.** It gives the most gain for the least loss. A consumer's regulation reads its facts with `source`; the process configuration keeps only the binding of source to cell. `synthese.rs` and `rijen.rs` go when every process is migrated. Lexostatuses stay in the reduction language during this step and are called through `source` as they are.
4. **Reduction per lexostatus.** A lexostatus entry in `cel.yaml` may name a regulation instead of a `reduction`. During the transition a cell can run both for the same request and log a difference. When a lexostatus matches on the whole comparison set, its `reduction` is removed.
5. **Removal.** When no `reduction` is left, `reductie`, the derivation checks of `controle.rs` and `lexostatus.json` go.

The list lexostatuses (`groepeer: zaakkenmerk`, the work queue) come last, since the experiment did not try them (Open Question 3). `zaakstand` stays a lexostatus the runtime offers (RFC-044 §4) until that is settled.

## Relation to the position paper

The paper puts reduction in the cell ("Reductie vindt altijd plaats ín de cel waar de betreffende chronolexogrammen zijn vastgelegd") and synthesis with the consumer ("Synthese gebeurt bij de afnemer, niet bij de bron"). This RFC keeps both. The cell still filters and derives over its own chronicles and nothing else, and the consumer still combines. Only the language of the derivation changes, which the paper does not prescribe; it describes the derivation as "een afleidingsproces" and leaves its form open.

The paper asks, as a research question, how actors can tell "wanneer wiskundige afleiding versus hermeneutische interpretatie van toepassing is". Writing the derivation in the law format is one answer. A reduction becomes a text a jurist can read next to the provision it rests on, with its legal ground on each derivation, and the difference between a sum and a reading is then visible in the same place as the reading. That answer is our own choice; the paper does not give it.

## Open Questions

1. **An instant type.** A law that compares moments within a day, or across time zones, needs a timestamp type with an offset. None in the corpus does yet. The experiment's fictional register had a notice at 08:30Z that falls after one at 09:00+01:00; the cell orders that correctly, and a comparison in the law would not.
2. **A `source` per element.** The vector pattern of §5 covers the proof of concept. A consumer that asks a different cell per row, chosen by a field of the row, would need a `source` inside a `FOREACH`.
3. **Grouping.** The list lexostatuses group grams per case and drop what a later gram cancels. That needs the distinct values of a field as a collection, which neither `FOREACH` nor this RFC offers.
4. **The trial reduction.** RFC-044 Open Question 4 asks how a process checks a draft before submitting. With reduction as an engine run, the process can run the cell's regulation over the chronicle plus the draft, without the cell recording anything. Whether that is still reduction in the paper's sense is open.
5. **Where a lexostatus regulation lives.** It is the cell's own regulation, and it reads like implementation policy of the actor who keeps the chronicle. Whether it sits in the corpus next to that actor's other regulations, or with the cell configuration, and whether it is published as law, is not decided.
6. **`zaakstand`.** The runtime offers it to every cell with cases. It could be a regulation the runtime ships, run like any other lexostatus, which would remove the last derivations in Rust.
