## Context

[RFC-022](/rfcs/rfc-022) §4.1 takes the paper's distinction between *chronolexoreductie* (a cell reduces its own chronicle to a lexostatus) and *chronolexosynthese* (a consumer combines lexostatussen from several cells). For the reduction it sketches a provisional shape: each cell declares its lexostatussen in cell configuration, `lexostatus_definitions` with a `reduction` block, and leaves the full format to a future RFC.

That future format was built twice before this RFC, and both times it became a rule language of its own.

The chronolex proof of concept (branch `poc/chronolex`, not for merging) wrote a lexostatus in `cell.yaml` with its own vocabulary: `filter`, `exists`, `sum`, `pick: latest`, `contains`, `year_of`, `group_by`. A fact was then written three times. The date of a notice was declared in the stream, derived again in a lexostatus, and mapped once more in the process configuration before it reached the article that read it. The compact cell in `packages/cel` started the same way, with `lexostatuses.yaml` for the application and for what was paid on a voorschot.

Such a language repeats what the law format already does. [RFC-016](/rfcs/rfc-016) gave the law format `FOREACH` with `filter` and `combine`, which covers filtering a list, testing whether something exists and adding up a field. A derivation in a cell's own language has no type checker ([RFC-037](/rfcs/rfc-037)), no trace per element ([RFC-039](/rfcs/rfc-039)) and no conformance suite. It also lives in cell configuration, where a jurist does not look.

### Experiment A

An experiment on 25 September 2026 (branch `exp/reductie-als-engine`, not for merging) ran every reduction of the proof of concept as an ordinary engine run: each lexostatus became a regulation whose input is the chronicle. The outcomes matched, 180 cases and 386 compared values without a difference. It was 50 to 100 times slower per reduction (0.21 ms against 2 µs, median, release build), and it showed what the engine lacks: an inner `FOREACH` cannot see the outer element, there is no way to build a record, no "latest", no local name inside a loop body, and "no gram" cannot be told apart from "a gram without that field". §5 to §13 below take up those gaps.

### Where the reading lives

The experiment answered the question of language and left the question of place open: its regulations had no actor behind them. Two rounds followed in the proof of concept. On 28 September the reading moved into the article that asks for a fact (`reads`), which put the query algorithm of one body's register in another body's law. On 29 September it moved to the keeper of the register, under the provisions that set up the register and allow it to be queried.

On 6 October 2026 the owner of this work settled it for the compact cell as well: reduction is business logic of whoever holds the facts, and it is written in the same rule language as the law, as an article in the holder's policy. What a law says is followed; what policy says is an article of that policy; what nobody says is written on behalf of the holder and marked as an assumption (§4).

## Decision

A fact from outside a regulation is a value from a source. The law says which register exists, who keeps it and who may ask; it never says how the register works. The query is an article in the implementing policy of the keeper, written in the law format and run by the engine. The consumer asks for it with an ordinary `source`. Which system answers is said only where the register is bound: for a chronicle, in the configuration of the cell that holds it.

This amends RFC-022 §4.1 on the place and form of a reduction. It keeps what §4.1 decided: the reduction stays with the holder of the facts, a consumer cannot inject a reduction of its own, and synthesis happens at the consumer.

§1 to §4 place the reading. §5 to §13 are the engine changes the placement needs, each argued on its own because a law needs several of them outside the chronolex runtime; none of them is built yet. §14 says where a query runs.

### 1. Four layers for a source

Each part of a source sits where a text carries it.

1. **A law sets up the register and names its keeper**, and says what goes into it. Kieswet G 1 lid 1: an association may ask the central electoral committee to enter its designation "in een register dat door het centraal stembureau wordt bijgehouden".
2. **A law gives the consumer the ground to ask.** Wet op de politieke partijen 121a: "Op verzoek van de Autoriteit verschaft een op grond van de Kieswet ingesteld centraal stembureau haar informatie betreffende de aanduidingen die bij hem geregistreerd zijn".
3. **The keeper's implementing policy describes the query**: its parameters, its outputs, and the search as engine actions.
4. **The consumer's policy fills in only what the law leaves open**, such as the key a register is queried on.

The query article has one input without a source, `source: {}`: the register. The consumer reads its outputs with an RFC-007 `source` to the keeper's policy, nothing new. The binding of the register to what answers it is not in any law or policy.

In this repository the holder and the consumer are often the same body. The Dienst Toeslagen reads back its own chronicle with `fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen`: article 1 gives the voorschot that holds for a berekeningsjaar (Awir 16 lid 1, 22 lid 1); article 2 the account from the application; article 3 what is still outstanding after a refused payment; article 3a what the bank credited on the voorschot (Awir 24 lid 2); article 4 the expected income, also for a following year; article 5 the latest inkomensgegeven over the year; article 6 the toekenning over the year, with what is left to pay or to recover; article 7 what of a nabetaling was ordered and not refused; article 8 the terugvordering and what of it was collected. Each article has the input `grams` with `source: {}`. The cell configuration binds it:

```yaml
# corpus/demo/cells/toeslagen/cell.yaml
registers:
  fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen#kroniek: {chronicle: toeslagen}
```

The key is `<policy>#<name of the register>`. The cell registers one engine data source per binding, scoped to that policy, and a policy reads at most one register of a cell. On a reading the source answers with the grams of the chronicle that hold at the moment of reading, one row per gram, in the order they hold (§7): the fields of the gram flattened, next to `id`, `event`, `type`, `subtype`, `stage`, `establishes`, `root`, `sequence`, `effective_at`, `effective_date`, `recorded_at`, `recorded_date` and `period`. A gram that holds only later is not in it. A field the law gives an event and a gram lacks is `null` in its row, so a policy can test for it.

The name of the register belongs to the keeper; a law such as the Kieswet says only "een register". Until the schema has a place for it (Open Question 3), it is the second half of the binding key.

### 2. Generic duties are carried out by the authority's own article

Awir 24 lid 2 sets off the voorschotten against the toegekende tegemoetkoming, and Awb 4:95 lid 4 does the same for subsidy advances. Neither says which administration holds the payments of one administrative body, and a general law cannot. The entry point is an article in the body's own policy (or its own law) that names the general provision as its `legal_basis` and reads the payment administration through a `source`. The general law gets no `open_terms` for it, and the engine does not choose an implementation by `competent_authority`.

In this repository: what was paid on the voorschot for a berekeningsjaar is an article of `fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen`, counted from the grams that record an installment as paid. Only what was paid counts, not what was granted: a granted installment that never arrived was not paid.

### 3. Gram identity and references

There is no case (*zaak*) as a separate record. The cell gives each gram an id when it records it (a UUID v7, so ids rise in time and are unique across cells), and a gram refers by id to the gram it belongs to. The name of each reference comes from the text that establishes it: a decision refers to the application it is taken on as `on_application` ("besluit op de aanvraag", the text [RFC-046](/rfcs/rfc-046) `decides_on` records); an installment refers to its `voorschot`, a bank's answer to the `betaalopdracht` it answers. The establishing article, or the derivation from it ([RFC-049](/rfcs/rfc-049)), declares the reference, and the cell refuses a gram whose required reference points at a gram that does not exist or was established by another article or stage.

A group follows from the references. The root of a gram is the gram without a reference that its references lead to, and a query selects on it: the row of every gram carries `root`. The group has no name of its own; it is called after its root, "application g-101". This keeps RFC-008's rule that the besluit itself is the state container, without the grouping key (`zaakkenmerk`) RFC-022 §1.2 still used.

### 4. The text test

Every part of a binding (the register, its keeper, its content, the key it is queried on, its name) gets one of three tags.

- **Text**: a law says it, with a quotation.
- **Policy**: a choice of the keeper or the consumer, written as machine-readable implementing policy with an owner.
- **Assumption**: nobody carries it. An assumption may stand in a proof of concept, visible and named.

We may write policy on behalf of an organization that has not adopted it. It is then marked as such, in its name, in its header and in every article, so it cannot pass for policy with an owner. `fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen` is written that way: its name says *aanname*, and each choice in it that does not follow from the law is marked. When the real keeper later publishes its own interface, the binding may run partly outside RegelRecht; the method allows that, because no law and no policy named the implementation.

### 5. A nested FOREACH sees the outer binding

*Proposed, not built.* An inner `FOREACH` can read the `as` binding of every `FOREACH` it is nested in. This replaces the scope rule of RFC-016, which gives the child context an empty local scope. A query joins (for each advance decision, its payments), and through `collection` alone that means building the outer element's matches first. To keep a law from changing meaning silently, a binding may not shadow another visible name, and the type checker runs over the corpus before the change lands.

### 6. Records

*Proposed, not built.* `{operation: RECORD, fields: {…}}` builds a record whose field values are law-format values, so a `FOREACH` without `combine` returns a table. A parameter, input or output of `type: object` may declare its `fields`, and one of `type: array` its `items`, so the type checker knows the type of `$g.bedrag`.

### 7. The latest element

*Partly built.* The cell gives the grams of a register in the order they legally hold: by `effective_at`, then by `recorded_at`, then in recording order. A fact recorded late about an earlier moment is therefore not the latest state. Each row carries its place in that order as `sequence`.

The law format has no "latest". A policy takes the latest gram of a selection as the `MAX` of `sequence`, and then reads one value from it with an `ADD` over that one gram. The proposal is `combine: FIRST` and `combine: LAST` on `FOREACH`, with an array input declaring the order its supplier guarantees (`ordered_by: [effective_at, recorded_at]`); a `FIRST` or `LAST` over a collection without one is a load error.

### 8. Local names in a FOREACH

*Proposed, not built.* An optional `let` on `FOREACH` binds names per element, so a subexpression is not written twice. A `let` name follows the shadowing rule of §5 and is a node in the trace.

### 9. Absent, null and empty

*Proposed, not built.* A query must tell three answers apart: no gram at all, a gram whose field is empty, and a gram with a value. `FOREACH` gains an optional `empty`, its value when no element passes the filter. With its `legal_basis` it says why "not in the register" means what it means: the keeper keeps the register, so what is not in it did not happen. Today the cell writes a missing field as `null` in a row (§1), which covers the second case and leaves the first to the identity of `combine`.

### 10. Sources per element

*Proposed, not built.* The engine does not get a `source` per element. A query a consumer needs per row takes a list of keys and returns a list of rows. A `source.parameters` value may evaluate to `null` when the target parameter is `nullable`.

### 11. The collection limit

*Proposed, not built.* `MAX_ARRAY_SIZE` becomes an engine setting that a runtime may raise and records in the receipt, and the data source receives the parameters of the query article as criteria, so it returns only matching records.

### 12. Checks against the stream

*Partly built.* The cell refuses a binding whose chronicle no stream of the cell records in, and a policy that reads more than one register of a cell, and it reports a register that is not bound with the engine. Checking every path a query reads against the fields the law gives an event waits for §6.

### 13. Trace and provenance

*Partly built.* A query produces an engine trace like any other. A value the cell reads with a policy names the register and the article the engine executed in its provenance (`{source: lexostatus, lexostatus: voorschot, register: fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen#kroniek, article: fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen#1}`: the lexostatus is the article's `endpoint`), and a reading returns the grams it read. An iteration node that names the id of the gram it visited waits for §6.

### 14. Where it runs

The query article belongs to the keeper. The runtime that serves the register runs it over that register and nothing else. For a register bound to a cell, the cell runs the engine on its keeper's query articles; it still never works across cells.

## Why

### Benefits

- One rule language. A reading is type-checked, traced and tested with the same tools as the article it serves, and a jurist reads it in the same format.
- Every part of a source sits where a text or an owner carries it, and the text test makes an unowned part visible. Law and policy name no system, so a register can move from a cell to an API without a change to either.
- The reduction language of the proof of concept (about 2,300 lines of Rust and 600 of schema there) is not needed in the compact cell.

### Tradeoffs

- The YAML is longer than a reduction language, by an estimated 1.5 to 2 times, and until §7 lands a "latest" takes two steps.
- An engine run is slower than a purpose-built reduction. For a proof of concept it does not matter; for a production register the criteria of §11 come first.
- A proof of concept holds policy nobody adopted. The marking of §4 is the only thing that tells it apart.

### Alternatives Considered

**A reduction language in cell configuration** (RFC-022 §4.1's provisional shape; the proof of concept's `cell.yaml`; the compact cell's first `lexostatuses.yaml`). Fast and short, and it says "no gram is no" in one word. Rejected as the end state: a second language for what `FOREACH` expresses, without type checking or trace, and outside what a jurist reviews.

**The lexostatus as a regulation of the cell** (Experiment A). It works, but nobody owns it.

**`reads` in the reading article** (28 September). It puts the query in the law of the consumer, which then prescribes how another body's register is searched.

**Inversion of control on the general law.** Awir 24 or Awb 4:95 would get `open_terms` for "the amount paid", and each body's policy would implement it. The engine selects an implementation only by municipality, province or water board code, so two national bodies would collide. The body's own article (§2) needs none of it.

## Implementation in this pull request

Built in `packages/cel` (PR #1689): the register binding in `cell.yaml` and the engine data source per register (§1, `register.rs`), the rows in legal order with `sequence` (§7), gram ids, references and roots (§3, `chronicle.rs`), the binding checks of §12, the provenance of §13, and the policy `fictief_beleid_kroniek_toeslagen`, marked as an assumption (§4). The engine changes of §5, §6 and §8 to §11 are not built. That a decision's inputs from the application need no reduction of their own is [RFC-047](/rfcs/rfc-047).

## Relation to the position paper

The paper puts reduction in the cell ("Reductie vindt altijd plaats ín de cel waar de betreffende chronolexogrammen zijn vastgelegd") and synthesis with the consumer ("Synthese gebeurt bij de afnemer, niet bij de bron"). Here the reduction is a query of the register, written by its keeper and run where the register is kept. The paper describes the derivation as "een afleidingsproces" and leaves its form open. Writing it in the law format is our choice, and so is placing it in the keeper's policy rather than in cell configuration; the paper does not ask for either.

The paper says nothing about the identity of a gram or a reference between grams. The id and the references of §3 are our own; the creator of Chronolexografie agreed to them on 29 September 2026.

## Open Questions

1. **Details of the engine language.** An instant type for a law that compares moments within a day, and grouping by distinct values.
2. **A `source` per element**, for a consumer that asks a different register per row.
3. **Is `register` a schema element?** Now the name is the second half of the binding key. A `register` block in the keeper's policy would say it in the policy itself, at the price of a schema change.
