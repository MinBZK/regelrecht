## Context

Every parameter, input, output and definition in a law declares a `type`
(`string`, `number`, `boolean`, `amount`, `date`, `array`, `object`) and may
carry a `type_spec` with a unit, a precision and bounds. Until now those
declarations were documentation. `just validate` checked the shape of the YAML
against the JSON schema and nothing else; the engine is dynamically typed and
looks at the value it receives, never at the declaration; `min` and `max` have
been "parsed metadata, not yet enforced" since they were added.

RFC-036 made the cost visible. It introduced a run-time distinction between an
absent value (`null`) and an unknown one, and a rule that calculating with an
absence is an error. That error is correct, and it arrives too late: a
register delivers `null` for a field the law never expected to be absent, the
value travels through two cross-law calls, and article 3 of a correct law is
told to add an absence test. Review of that RFC put the finger on it: whether a
value may be absent belongs in the type, where the validator can see it, not
in the data, where only the engine sees it.

Adding one flag does not need a type checker. Enforcing it does: to refuse
`EQUALS $huur null` on a field that is not nullable, the validator has to know
what `$huur` refers to and what its declaration says. Once that environment
exists, the rest of the declarations can be checked at the same cost.

## Decision

The engine gets a static type checker over the expressions of a law. It runs
in two places with one implementation (`packages/engine/src/typecheck.rs`): in
the `validate` binary after the schema check, so `just validate` and the
pre-commit hook enforce it on every law in the corpus, and in
`LawExecutionService::load_law`, so a law that fails the check cannot be
loaded, by the editor's WASM engine or anyone else. A finding names the law,
the article, the output or action, and the rule.

**Environment.** For each article the checker knows the declared type and
nullability of every parameter, input and output, the type of every definition
from its literal, and that `$referencedate` is a non-nullable date. Property
paths into objects (`$record.field`) and `FOREACH` bindings have no known type.
The checker never reports on what it does not know: a false positive on a
correct law is a bug in the checker.

**Rules for absence** (the rules of RFC-036, run here):

- **N1** A comparison with the literal `null` needs a nullable field on the
  other side. `EQUALS $huur null` on a non-nullable `huur` cannot be true and
  is rejected.
- **N2** The literal `null` appears only as the value of a nullable output,
  directly or as a branch of an `IF` that is that output's value, or as an
  element of a `LIST` or the body of a `FOREACH` without `combine` (an array
  of absences is a value). As an operand, or as the value of a non-nullable
  output, it is rejected.
- **N3** An `IF` without `default` yields `null` when no case matches. That is
  allowed only as the value of a nullable output; nested inside an operation,
  or as the value of a non-nullable output, it is rejected.
- **N4** A nullable variable enters arithmetic, an ordering, `AND`, `OR`,
  `NOT`, an `IF` condition, a date operation or a `FOREACH` collection only
  on a path where it is known to be non-null (the subject of `IN` is exempt:
  membership of an absence is structural under RFC-036 and cannot fail): in the `then`
  of `NOT(EQUALS $x null)`, in the cases after `EQUALS $x null` and in the
  `default`, and in the conditions of an `AND` after `NOT(EQUALS $x null)` (of
  an `OR` after `EQUALS $x null`). Elsewhere it is rejected. Passing it on, to
  another law, into `EQUALS`, or straight to an output, is fine.
- **N5** An input that takes a nullable output from another law and is not
  nullable itself is rejected, when that law is available to the checker.

**Rules for types:**

- **T1** Arithmetic and ordering take `number` or `amount` operands; `ADD`
  also joins two strings or two arrays (RFC-007), ordering also compares two
  dates (RFC-021).
- **T2** `AND`, `OR`, `NOT` and an `IF` condition take `boolean` operands.
- **T3** `EQUALS` between two known, different types is rejected; `number`
  against `amount` is allowed, and `date` against `string`, because dates
  arrive as ISO strings.
- **T4** A literal assigned to an output must have the output's type.

Each rule applies only where the operand types are known. Rules that fire on
a correct law in `corpus/regulation` or `corpus/demo` are narrowed until they
do not; the integration test that runs the checker over both corpora and
expects zero findings is what keeps the corpus typed from here on.

## Why

- **The error lands where the mistake is.** An author who forgets that a
  register may deliver "none" hears it from `just validate` while writing the
  law, with the field named, instead of from a run-time error in another law.
- **Declarations become contracts.** A binding that says a register may deliver
  `null` (`absent: null` in the demo's `bindings.yaml`) and a law that says the
  field is never absent now contradict each other visibly, and a test can say
  so.
- **The pipeline gets a gate.** Laws written by the enrichment pipeline are
  checked by the same rules as laws written by hand; a generated `EQUALS …
  null` on a field nobody declared nullable does not reach the corpus.
- **`min`, `max` and units are next.** The environment the checker builds is
  what those need too; they are out of scope here and named as the next
  candidates.

### Tradeoffs

- **A checker is code that can be wrong.** A too-eager rule rejects a correct
  law. The mitigation is the zero-findings integration test over the corpus
  and the discipline that a rule is narrowed, never that a law is bent to it.
- **Two type systems.** The checker reasons about declarations; the engine
  reasons about values. They agree by construction on what is checked, and the
  run-time rules of RFC-036 stay as the net for what the checker cannot see
  (an empty collection's `MAX`, a property inside an object).
- **The corpus has to declare.** Every field a law tests for `null` gains a
  `nullable: true`. That is an honest annotation, not a change in logic, and it
  was done in the same change.

### Open point: a dedicated fallback operation

An `IF_ABSENT` operation (`subject: $x`, `value: <fallback>`, typed as
nullable `T` to `T`) would give the checker one place where a nullable value
becomes a value, and would read like the legal phrase "bij gebreke van".
RFC-004 adds an operation when a real law needs it. The laws in both corpora
test absence as a boolean ("heeft partner", "is beëindigd") and rarely fall
back to a value, so the flow rule N4 over the existing `IF` and `EQUALS …
null` covers them. The operation is added the day a legal text says "bij
gebreke van X geldt Y", not before.

### Alternatives Considered

- **Only the flag, enforced at run time.** The engine could refuse `null` for
  a non-nullable input when it resolves it, and nothing more. That catches the
  data error at the boundary but leaves `EQUALS $huur null` on a non-nullable
  field as a comparison that is always false, silently. The validator has to
  read the expression.
- **Nullable types instead of a flag** (`type: number?` or a union with
  `null`). Equivalent in power; the flag keeps the `type` enum and the
  `type_spec` machinery untouched and is one line in the schema.
- **A separate lint tool.** Would drift from the engine's own understanding of
  the language. One implementation in the engine crate, called from the
  validator and the loader, cannot.

## Consequences

- Engine: `packages/engine/src/typecheck.rs`; called from `bin/validate.rs`
  and from `load_law`; `NullForNonNullable` and `NullOutput` errors at run
  time (RFC-036).
- Schema: `nullable` on `baseField` in v0.5.8 (see RFC-036).
- Corpus: nullable declarations added where laws test for `null`; the
  integration test keeps both corpora at zero findings.
- Editor: the scenario editor offers a `null` cell only for nullable columns;
  type-check errors from `loadLaw` are shown to the author.
- Next: `min`/`max` (issue #444) and unit consistency (RFC-023) as rules of the
  same checker.
